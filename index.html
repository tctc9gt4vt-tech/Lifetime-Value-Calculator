How the model works

What it does. The model follows every handset sold, by region and launch year, from its first sale to the end of its last life, and adds up the value it generates along the way: the sale itself, finance interest, airtime, trade-ins and refurbished resale, private sales, and material recovery at recycling, less the costs of subsidies, trade-in payouts, refurbishment, logistics and collection. The same engine runs for smartphones, tablets, smartwatches and laptops.

Where the numbers come from. The tool opens on the FDM PULSE standard: a scenario built from the PULSE ACPBF, Forecast Summary and Quarterly Volume files, one per device. From PULSE directly: new units and prices by region and year, the brand mix and each brand’s price (Apple, Samsung, Others), and the channel split of trade-ins and refurbished sales. Worked out to match PULSE: trade-in, private-sale and resale shares, resale and informal price levels, refurb flows between regions, and each brand’s trade-in and import behaviour. Still illustrative (not from PULSE): airtime ARPU and plan mix, finance and subsidy terms, lifetimes, refurbishment and logistics costs, material values and the share of service revenue credited to the handset. Check these before quoting any output that depends on them.

Colour coding. On the input tabs, blue cells came from the PULSE upload, amber cells were adjusted to fit PULSE, and dashed purple cells are extrapolated for years PULSE doesn’t cover. Unshaded cells are your inputs or the defaults. Typing in a shaded cell makes it yours.

Regions and brands follow PULSE: eleven regions (North America, Latin America, UK, Rest of Western Europe, Eastern Europe, Middle East, Africa, China, India, APAC Developed = Australia and Japan, APAC Emerging = Indonesia and the rest of APAC) and three brands (Apple, Samsung, Others; for laptops, Lenovo, HP and Dell sit in Others). Each brand has its own tier mix and behaviour. Price tiers (premium, mid-range, budget) shape how devices behave; with brand prices on, what each device sells for comes from its brand’s PULSE price.

How devices move. Each year’s sales are spread evenly through the year and followed month by month. When an owner is done, the device is traded in, sold privately, passed on, put in a drawer, recycled or lost, in the shares set on the Routes tabs. Trade-ins are either resold as refurbished (in the region of trade-in or exported, per the Refurb flows tab) or recycled. Paths that end in the same place at the same time are merged, so every combination of routes, regions and owners is covered. After the maximum number of owners, devices go to recycling or loss.

Value. New sales are units × price. Finance interest uses an amortising loan on the financed share at the blended APR. Airtime is devices in use × the plan-weighted ARPU of the region they are in × the share credited to the handset; new and second-hand owners have separate plan mixes. Resale prices are the launch price × (1 − annual value decline)age × the region’s resale price index; informal (private) prices also take the informal price index. Trade-in payouts are a share of the refurbished price. Present value discounts every year to the stated price year.

Choosing what counts as value. The Value includes switches above the results leave streams out of every value figure, chart and comparison: new device sales, trade-ins & resale, airtime & service, finance interest and material recovery, plus all costs at once. Each stream takes its own costs with it (subsidies with device sales; trade-in payouts, refurbishment and logistics with trade-ins & resale; collection with material recovery). Unit volumes, device flows, the channel view and the check against PULSE don’t change. The choice is saved with the scenario, and comparisons use the current scenario’s choice for both sides.

Check against PULSE and calibration. The check compares what the model produces in each region and year (new units, trade-ins, refurbished and informal used sales, their prices, and Apple’s and Samsung’s share of refurbished sales) with PULSE. Calibrate to PULSE adjusts the worked-out inputs launch year by launch year, weighted by how much each launch year contributes to each calendar year, until the gaps close; the standard has already been calibrated. Where a region sells more refurbished devices in PULSE than its own trade-ins could supply, calibration opens a small import route from regions with trade-ins to spare. Because the standard is fitted to PULSE, the check shows how well it fits, not an independent test.

Where PULSE has no data. Sales before 2021 are backcast from PULSE’s 2021 figures using industry shipment history (IDC; China and India separately for smartphones). Brand mix, brand prices and channel splits outside PULSE’s years follow each series’ recent PULSE trend, fading out over a few years. Trade-ins after PULSE’s last actual year are estimated from PULSE’s refurbished sales forecast and each region’s trade-in-to-refurbished ratio, and marked ≈ in the check.

The standard and new PULSE files. Anyone can reload the standard for any device. Uploading new PULSE files offers two choices: use them in the current scenario only, or replace the team standard, which reads every device, calibrates each, and makes it the starting point for everyone. Your own scenarios are never overwritten.

Inputs by year. Most inputs can be given values for chosen years; years in between are interpolated in a straight line, and values hold flat before the first and after the last year set. Brand, price, buying, route, resale and flow settings follow each device’s launch year for its whole life. Plan mix and ARPU follow the calendar year the device is in use.

History, forecast and launch years. Years up to the last year of actual data are shaded as history. The launch-year view shows the full lifetime value of each year’s sales and how much had been realised by the end of the actual data. The industry view by calendar year only includes devices sold from the first sales year, so it understates the early years (older devices still in use are not counted) and falls away after the last sales year.

Impacts are events layered on a scenario: each changes chosen levers by a percentage at full strength, with a timing shape. Sales, price, buying, trade-in, lifetime and value-decline effects follow the launch year; ARPU effects follow the calendar year. The impact results compare the scenario with and without them. Comparison runs another scenario through the same engine and filters.
